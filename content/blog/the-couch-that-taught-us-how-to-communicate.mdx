When Jurie and I first moved in together, we discovered that our L-shaped couch — you know, *that* couch, the comfortable one that seems to mold perfectly to your body — was about to become the unlikely setting for a lesson neither of us expected.

We had our spots. The left side was Jurie's favorite. The right side was mine. Simple enough, right?

## The Daily Dance

Except it wasn't that simple. Here's what happened, over and over again:

Jurie would be watching TV, sitting comfortably on the left side — his favorite spot, his preferred corner. I'd walk into the living room ready to relax, heading toward the right side where I liked to sit. And every single time — *every single time* — the moment I arrived, he would get up and move to the right side. My side. My favorite spot.

Not begrudgingly. Not with an annoyed sigh. He'd just get up and move over, leaving his comfortable spot empty and taking over mine.

At first, I thought maybe I was imagining it. But no. This happened again and again. He'd sit in his comfortable spot on the left, I'd come in, and he'd abandon it to come sit on my side.

## The Frustration

Naturally, I started to get frustrated. Why did he keep moving to my favorite part of the couch when I walked in? It made absolutely no sense to me. We had a whole L-shaped couch. He had his side that he clearly preferred. Why did he have to move to my side the second I showed up instead of just... staying where he was comfortable?

Was he trying to piss me off? Was this some weird power play? Did he resent that I was there and want to invade my space to show it?

I found myself annoyed every single time, wondering why he couldn't just stay where he was. He had his side. I had mine. It wasn't complicated. But he kept doing it anyway — abandoning his comfortable spot to encroach on mine the moment I appeared.

I never said anything about it. I just... stewed. Quietly. Like you do.

## The Conversation That Changed Everything

Eventually, I couldn't hold it in anymore. I asked him, point-blank: "Why do you always move to my favorite side when I come to sit? Why don't you just stay where you are?"

And his answer completely shifted how I understood the moment.

He looked confused. "What do you mean? I do stay where I'm comfortable. When I'm sitting on the left by myself, that's where I want to be. But when you come in, I move to the right because I want to give you the best spot. I thought that was your favorite side, so I figured you'd want to sit there."

I just... stopped.

Wait. He was moving to my side thinking he was *giving* it to me? Thinking he was being generous? He wasn't encroaching on my space to annoy me — he was abandoning his own comfort in what he thought was an act of kindness.

All that frustration, all those moments of feeling annoyed or confused or like he was deliberately trying to annoy me — it had all been rooted in a complete misunderstanding. He wasn't being inconsiderate. He was being kind, even if it was kind in a way that completely backfired. He was trying to give me something good. He just didn't realize that I didn't need him to give me anything — I just wanted him to stay comfortable in his own spot and let me enjoy mine.

## The Real Lesson

This silly couch situation became one of the clearest illustrations of something absolutely fundamental: you can't assume you know what someone else wants.

It's so easy to interpret someone's actions through the lens of our own preferences, our own logic, our own hurt feelings. I assumed he wanted to frustrate me because I couldn't fathom why else he'd do what he was doing. But I was wrong.

The solution wasn't for me to silently suffer or build resentment. It was to ask. To have the conversation. To say out loud: "Hey, this is what's happening from my perspective, and I'm confused."

Because here's the thing about moving in with someone, or being in a relationship with someone, or honestly just being around someone regularly: you don't have the same preferences. What feels like the obvious, best choice to one person might be completely different for another. And that's okay. But only if you talk about it.

## Now

Now we both laugh about it. Jurie sits on the left. I sit on the right. No switching. No confusion. Just two people enjoying their own favorite spots, together.

But more importantly, we know that when something feels off, when we don't understand why someone is doing something, the answer isn't to assume the worst. It's to ask. Even when it seems like an obvious slight, even when it feels intentionally annoying — sometimes the explanation is just someone trying to love you in a way that doesn't quite land.

Communication isn't just about big fights or serious conversations. Sometimes it's about sitting down and saying, "I noticed you always move when I come over, and I'm confused about why."

And sometimes the answer is the most loving thing you could hear — even if it's also the most bewildering.
