I love learning new things.

I have a background in business. I have my personal training certificates. I have training from several institutions, and more certificates than I'd like to count. If there's a certificate for it, I've probably wanted it. I'm not embarrassed about that. I genuinely enjoy the moment of finishing something new.

But I've noticed a problem. I know more than I use.

We all have access to almost anything now. Every question has an answer, every goal has a course, and every habit has a podcast explaining why it works. That's an incredible thing. It's also a trap I keep falling into.

## Watching feels like doing

My husband put it simply: because we have access to so much, our brains start to think we're making progress when we watch something or research something. We feel like we're accomplishing things. But we're not bored enough to actually stop and do them.

I think he's onto something. Some days I can spend an hour learning about sleep, or building a routine, or planning a goal, and by the end I feel like I've done something. Nothing in my life has changed.

I don't think the answer is to stop learning. I think the answer is to notice when learning has become a way of avoiding the next step.

## What I already know

Here's the frustrating part. I already know what would help me. I know what I'd do to sleep better. I know how to build a routine. I know what I need to do to look after my health. I just keep choosing other things first.

Knowing isn't the same as doing. Doing is a little uncomfortable. It takes time, it might not work the first time, and it asks you to be ordinary for a while. Researching is easy. Nobody's watching, and nothing is at stake.

## My house is the same story

I love thrift shopping. I love buying something new, bringing it home, and finding a place for it. For a long time that felt like a small joy. But our house has more than it can hold, and I can see that now.

When life was calmer, I used to revamp one room at a time. I'd finish it, then move on to the next. It worked because I only had one thing to focus on.

This time I didn't. With my business, my work, my family and my friends all on my mind, I took on the whole house at once. Now everything is out at the same time, and I'm trying to catch up with a project I made far too big for the space I had to give it.

What I should have done is what I'm trying to do now: start with one room, finish it, and then move on. And use one in, one out, so I stop adding things back in.

My plan is to get rid of the extras, and keep only what fits our space and the drawers and cupboards we already have. I don't want to add anything more to the house. Once the excess is gone, one in, one out will keep it from filling up again.

## What I'm trying instead

I'm not going to wait until I feel fully motivated, because I'm not sure that will come. Here's what I'm trying:

- **Pick one thing I already know.** Not a new course. Something I've already studied, like a wind-down routine or a simple movement habit.
- **Do the smallest version of it for seven days.** Small enough that I can't talk myself out of it.
- **Track it with one tick a day.** Nothing fancy. Just a visible record that I did it.
- **Set a rule for new learning.** One thing from my list has to be running before I start another course or certificate. I call it one in, one out.

I'm not promising this will fix everything. I'm promising to do the smallest real thing today, and then do it again tomorrow.

## If this sounds like you

Maybe you have a folder of saved articles, a shelf of books you've started, or a long list of things you'd like to change. Maybe you're great at getting ready to start.

You don't need another resource to begin. Pick one thing you already know, and do the smallest version of it today. Then tell me what happened. I'd love to hear it.
