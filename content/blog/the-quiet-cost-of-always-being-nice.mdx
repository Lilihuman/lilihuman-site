For most of my life, I believed I had one job in every room I walked into: make sure everyone left it feeling better than when they came in.

If the mood dipped, I lifted it. If two people bristled at each other, I smoothed the space between them. If someone seemed even slightly off, I took it as a personal assignment to fix it. I wanted everyone I spoke to — the cashier, the cousin, the stranger in line — to have the *best day ever*. I genuinely thought that was kindness.

It took me a long time to admit what was underneath it. I didn't believe I was enough on my own. Somewhere along the way I decided that unless I was useful, unless I was helpful, unless I was the one keeping the peace, no one would have a reason to keep me around. So I made myself indispensable to everyone, which is a polite way of saying I made myself responsible for everyone.

> I called it being nice. But a lot of it was fear wearing a friendly face.

## When kindness runs on fear

Here is what no one tells you about being too nice: it doesn't actually run on love. It runs on a quiet, constant anxiety. You're not giving because you're full. You're giving because you're afraid of what happens if you stop. And the math never works out, because you can pour and pour into rooms full of people and still walk away empty, wondering why you feel so unseen by the very people you tried hardest to please.

## A boundary is not a wall

What I'm learning, slowly, is that a boundary is not a wall and it is not unkindness. A boundary is simply me deciding that my health and my energy are not an unlimited resource I owe to whoever asks. It is me admitting that I cannot keep the peace for everyone, and that trying to was never my job in the first place.

And when I stop spending myself on every room, something surprising happens. There is finally something left for the people who actually matter — the ones who lift me up, who are honest with me, who make my life softer and bigger and better. Those people were never asking me to perform. They just wanted me. The real me, not the tireless, smiling version that's always on.

> I am important too. I'm still getting used to how that sentence feels in my mouth.

I'm not perfect at this. I still feel the old pull to fix the mood, to apologize for taking up space, to make myself useful so I'll be wanted. But I'm starting to believe that the people worth keeping won't need me to earn my place with them. They'll just be glad I'm there.

Being kind was never the problem. Abandoning myself to do it was. And I'm finally done paying that price.
