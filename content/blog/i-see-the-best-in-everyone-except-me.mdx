I'm the friend people call.

When someone I love is stuck, or hurting, or spiraling over a decision, I usually know exactly what to say. I can find the thread of light in almost any situation. I can look at a person the rest of the world has quietly given up on and still see the good in them — the soft part, the reason they became who they are, the version of them that's worth rooting for. It comes naturally to me. Honestly, it might be the thing I'm best at.

So here's the part I don't say out loud very often: I can't seem to do it for myself.

I give grace to everyone but me. I hand it out like it's endless — and then I turn around and give myself nothing but the bill. I feel like I'm failing. Like I'm not doing enough, for my family, for my work, for the people who count on me, for *me*. And then I get in my head about the smallest things. Tiny, teenie things that do not matter, that no one else even noticed, that I somehow let grow in the dark until they feel like evidence in a case I'm building against myself.

If you'd looked at my day from the outside, you'd probably have seen someone showing up. Doing the things. Being there for people. But inside, I was keeping a running list of everywhere I'd fallen short.

Then someone asked me a question I still can't shake:

*"If you listened to yourself the way you listen to a friend — what would you say?"*

And it stopped me cold. Because I knew, immediately, that I would never talk to a friend the way I talk to myself.

If a friend came to me and said, *"I feel like I'm failing, I'm not doing enough, I keep messing up the little things"* — I wouldn't pile on. I wouldn't agree with her worst thoughts and hand her more. I'd get quiet and gentle. I'd remind her of everything she's carrying that she's not giving herself any credit for. I'd tell her that being tired isn't the same as failing. That the fact she's worried about not doing enough is usually a sign she's doing plenty. I'd see the best in her — the way I always do — and I'd mean it.

I would never leave a friend alone in the dark with her harshest inner voice. And yet that's exactly where I've left myself, over and over.

I don't think I'm alone in this. I think a lot of us are fluent in a kindness we only ever speak to other people. We're generous, patient, forgiving — right up until the mirror. Then suddenly the rules change, and the person who deserves the least compassion is the one looking back.

I'm not writing this because I've fixed it. I haven't. I still catch myself spiraling over something that won't matter by next week. But now I have one small thing I didn't have before — a question I can ask myself in the middle of it:

*What would I say if this were my friend?*

Sometimes that's all it takes to loosen the grip. Not to make the hard feeling disappear, but to change the voice I'm hearing it in. To trade the critic for the friend. To offer myself even a fraction of the grace I've never had trouble giving anyone else.

So maybe that's the practice. Not to suddenly love every part of ourselves, but to become our own friend — the same steady, generous, best-in-you friend we already know how to be for everyone else.

The next time you're lying awake, replaying the tiny thing, being harder on yourself than you'd ever be on someone you love — try it. Ask yourself what you'd tell a friend.

And then, for once, actually listen.
